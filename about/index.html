The project

About Early Music Concerts

Early Music Concerts documents public performances of medieval and Renaissance music between 1915 and 1960, when musicological research and performance were unusually closely connected.

Public performance and the early music revival

Interest in medieval and Renaissance music accelerated after the First World War. Accounts of this revival have often emphasized music-making in university seminars, where scholars and students encountered unfamiliar repertories by singing and playing them. Public concerts were equally important. Musicologists, students, professional musicians, and amateur ensembles brought this music before audiences in churches, museums, lecture halls, and concert venues across Europe and the United States.

The years 1915–1960 capture a distinctive period in that history. Public performances became substantially more common after the First World War, while the postwar decades saw early music develop into a larger international movement. The project concentrates on the years in which academic research, editing, and concert performance most visibly informed one another.

What the programs preserve

Concert programs provide evidence that narrative histories cannot recover on their own. They identify locations, ensembles, directors, performers, and speakers. Many also cite the manuscripts, early prints, transcriptions, or modern editions used to prepare a performance. Reviews and related archival documents sometimes record how audiences and critics understood what they heard.

These documents are rarely gathered in one place. They survive among personal papers, university records, ensemble archives, libraries, and other collections. This website connects those dispersed materials. When reproduction is permitted, each concert record places an image of the surviving program beside standardized, searchable data while preserving the wording printed in the historical document.

Questions the database can answer

Bringing the programs together makes it possible to study how a broad repertory became a more selective performance canon; how particular composers and pieces gained prominence; and how local, national, and international networks shaped concert life. The records also restore performers, students, and other participants—many of them absent from existing histories—to the account of the early music revival.

The project combines quantitative analysis with close reading. Maps and chronological views show where and when repertories circulated, while individual records retain the archival context needed to interpret those patterns. The database will continue to grow as additional programs are located and catalogued.

Project history and support

Benjamin Ory developed Early Music Concerts through archival research and the construction of a relational database linking concerts, works, composers, performers, musical sources, editions, and archival holdings. Research supported by the American Musicological Society’s Ora Frishberg Saloman Fund enabled an extensive series of archival visits in 2023. The project continues as part of an FWO postdoctoral fellowship at KU Leuven (project 1219125N).
